\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deployed language-model agents are specialists
\citep{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,jimenez2024swebench,liu2024agentbench}:
a coding agent lives on code synthesis, a retrieval assistant on
multi-hop question answering, and both pay for every token. The model that serves such a workload is usually a reference model
scaled down by pruning, quantization, or distillation, and choosing the route and its
strength today means running each candidate through capability
benchmarks. A single compression ratio cannot replace that search, because
compression does not shrink capabilities uniformly: across twelve
public models, the same pruning density leaves QA the least-damaged
capability in some series and the most-damaged in others
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_hetero}).

We therefore ask how the capability-conditioned loss after compression
can be predicted from the initial model state and the configuration of
the method. The endpoint is the conditional loss of each capability on
a fixed probe set, written relative to a reference model measured beforehand, the
source for pruning and quantization and the initial student for
distillation (Eq.~\ref{eq:interface}). The admitted inputs are the initial state (parameter count,
pretraining tokens where disclosed, reference capability losses) and
the intervention setting; no measurement of the compressed target
enters. The relations are developed on a controlled panel of
Pythia checkpoints whose size and pretraining stage vary
independently, by comparing low-dimensional forms that use the same
inputs, and tested by freezing coefficients and predictions before new
configurations and states are measured. This separates whether the inputs help, whether a form adds anything
over a same-input alternative, and how far the relation transfers.

The outcome is a set of method-specific relations with measured
ranges. For pruning, a five-parameter power form with a
source-conditioned amplitude predicts math and code at unseen
densities inside the fitted range, ties a per-density regression, and
fails beyond the range and for QA. For quantization, bit-width alone
supports only a per-bit source regression, and with the group size as a second axis a frozen same-input
interpolation predicts unseen group sizes on the development states,
where a parametric surface loses. For distillation, under a fixed protocol a zero-at-zero reuse model beats its development-designated baseline for code at unseen data pools on both development students and a joint budget and pool model does so for QA on one, neither is the best of the forms frozen alongside it in hindsight, and transfer to a larger student remains unreliable. Our
contributions are (1) the prediction problem, its admitted inputs, and
a development-then-validation design that separates input value, form
value, and transfer range; (2) the per-method relations, their
coefficients, and their independent tests against same-input and
source-free baselines, with the capabilities and settings they cover
and where they stop; and (3) a connection of these relations to an explicit capability-
and budget-specific selection problem, validated retrospectively on
held-out states and prospectively, for one locked rule, on four fresh
states, with measurements, registered predictions, and code released.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

What can be predicted now is specific. Given the initial-state inputs
of a source in the development range, the pruning form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:power} predicts the math and code
response at any density inside the fitted range to within a few
tenths of a nat. Given the same inputs, a frozen same-input interpolation over the
measured grid predicts the grouped quantizer at unseen group sizes,
for the states it was fit on.
Given a student's state, a linear form predicts its math transfer,
and under a fixed distillation protocol a response model in budget,
reuse, and a student descriptor predicts unseen data pools on the two
development students. Each comes with coefficients and a tested range, and
\S\ref{sec:selection} connects them to an explicit selection problem.

What cannot be predicted is equally specific. No source-conditioned form predicts the QA response to pruning on a
new checkpoint, no form transfers to density 0.55, and on new sizes
(6.9B, and 2.8B inside the range) the source-free median curve is the
better predictor. Bit-width alone supports no continuous quantization relation. Joint
transfer of the distillation forms to a larger student and a new pool
is unreliable, and the distillation QA gain replicates on a fresh sample of the
primary distribution while reversing sign on an independent multi-hop
set, so it is a prediction relation on the teacher-matched
distribution and no general QA gain (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_measure}).

The dense capability anchor is the input to keep, the working forms
are low-dimensional, and transfer across sizes is bounded by the
development range. The limitations are small correlated panels, one
quantizer family and one teacher, undetected code specificity on a
secondary benchmark, and nominal storage as the only selection cost.

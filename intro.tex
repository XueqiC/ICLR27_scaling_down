\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deployed LLM-based agents are specialists. A coding agent depends on code
synthesis, debugging, and tool use; a retrieval assistant depends on
multi-hop question answering; neither needs frontier-scale generality,
and both are billed by inference cost. The practical question of the
small-model era is therefore not ``how good can an $N$-parameter model
be?'' but ``what is the cheapest model whose \emph{capability profile}
covers this workload?'' The standard route to such a model starts from a
strong dense source and scales it \emph{down}: pruning, quantization, or
distillation, usually followed by recovery training.

Choosing among these routes is currently guesswork or grid search.
Compression does not shrink all capabilities uniformly: at equal size,
memory, or theoretical compute, different methods preserve different
capabilities, improve some while collapsing others, hit cliffs at
different strengths, and recover differently under continued training
(\S\ref{sec:exp}). Aggregate perplexity, average benchmark scores, and
effective parameter counts do not reveal whether the capabilities an
agent needs survive. The full search space (source model $\times$
method $\times$ strength $\times$ recipe $\times$ recovery
budget) must today be explored by running every configuration through
capability benchmarks and agent evaluations, which is expensive and still
prone to missing sign flips, non-monotonicities, and collapse cliffs.

This paper develops \emph{predictive scaling-down laws} to replace that
search. The target map is
\begin{equation}
\big(\underbrace{\text{source state}}_{\mathbf x_0},\;
\underbrace{\text{common coordinates}}_{\mathbf z_m},\;
\underbrace{\text{method controls}}_{\boldsymbol\theta_m}\big)
\;\longmapsto\;
\underbrace{\text{capability vector}}_{\hat{\mathbf L}^{(m)}},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
followed by a workload link $S_W = G_W(\hat{\mathbf L},\mathbf r_W)$ and
the deployment decision
$\arg\min_{m,\theta_m} C_m(\theta_m;H)$ subject to
$G_W(\hat{\mathbf L}^{(m)},\mathbf r_W)\ge S_W^{\min}$, where $H$ is the
hardware environment, $\mathbf r_W$ the workload's capability
requirement, and $C_m$ the deployment-plus-recovery cost. Three design
rules keep the laws usable. First, \textbf{prediction-time inputs must be
observable before compression}: public source descriptors and an
\emph{observed dense capability anchor}
$\mathbf L_0^{\mathrm{obs}}$ that absorbs unknown data quality,
optimization, and post-training history, so predictions take the form
$L_{c,0}^{\mathrm{obs}}+\widehat{\Delta L}_c^{(m)}$. Gradient and Fisher
quantities serve as \emph{mechanism evidence}, not as prediction inputs.
Second, the observable is a \emph{vector} of continuous
capability-conditional losses (\S\ref{sec:prelim}), because a decade of
downstream-predictability results shows accuracy-space fitting destroys
the regularity laws need \citep{schaeffer2024predicting,
gadre2024language,bhagia2024establishing}. Third, \textbf{cross-method
unification is a hypothesis to test, not an assumption}: we compare a
fully shared law, a hierarchical shared-plus-corrections law, and
method-specific laws by held-out prediction error
(\S\ref{sec:claims}), and if unification fails, the method-selection
map is the central deliverable rather than an appendix.

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
Across twelve dense checkpoints from four model families (Qwen3,
Gemma-3/4, Muse, OLMo-3; 270M--32B):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Capability responses to compression are anisotropic with
an invariant ordering.} Multi-hop QA is the most pruning-robust
capability and math/code the most fragile in every model measured,
while magnitudes and signs are family-specific: QA in Qwen3 and
OLMo-3-32B \emph{improves} under substantial pruning, whereas QA in
Gemma never does. Sign, cliff location, and family identity all matter
to method choice.
\item \textbf{Capability-conditioned prediction targets real
structure.} Independent benchmarks of the same skill share gradient
signatures that anti-align across skills (seven models, three
families); in a first single-model test, zeroing one capability's
exclusive $0.05\%$ of coordinates selectively removed that capability
(near-diagonal ablation matrix). Multi-model control ablations are in
progress before stronger causal language is warranted.
\item \textbf{The collapse cliff is real, proportional, and
non-perturbative in loss}, yet a geometric retention statistic decays
smoothly and crosses a family-consistent critical value at the cliff,
which suggests that the cliff boundary can be carried by one constant per
family rather than fitted per model. \preliminary{three models, two
families}
\item \textbf{The other arms echo the structure.} Quantization is
capability-selective in the same directions with a collapse at 3 bits
in all nine models measured; distillation recipes that strip
chains of thought selectively damage math (paired early runs);
recovery training follows a saturating law whose asymptotic residual
under a fixed recipe separates redeemable from persistent damage, and
capability-aligned recovery data heals faster at small budgets but
re-damages under over-training.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Central thesis.}
The per-capability conditional loss vector serves as the common
prediction endpoint for all three arms, and the arms share one
information budget and one validation standard; the response form and
its range of validity are then determined separately for each arm by
evidence. Laws using only resource ratios fail to extrapolate across
the methods we tested, which negates the tested resource-ratio models
but leaves open whether some other single form unifies the arms.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) A capability-conditioned scaling-down formulation: predict a
multidimensional post-compression capability profile from observable
source state, common compression coordinates, and method-specific
controls, anchored at the observed dense capability
(\S\ref{sec:prelim}). (2) Observable-input, method-specific predictive
laws for pruning, quantization, and distillation (plus recovery), each
reported by held-out prediction error rather than in-sample fit
(\S\ref{sec:method},\S\ref{sec:exp}), including a controlled two-axis
test over source state and intervention strength with frozen
predictions and same-budget baselines (\S\ref{sec:twoaxes}). (3) A demonstration of
cross-method \emph{non-equivalence} on tested storage/active coordinates
alongside a shared capability interface (the per-capability loss
endpoint), adjudicated by leave-one-method/family/size-out tests. (4) A
discussion of how these predictors would enter a capability- and
cost-aware method-selection map, with the existing Pareto and
mis-ranking analyses as implications of prediction error rather than a
validated selector.
Dense-reference scaling and Fisher-implementation optimization are
\emph{not} claimed as contributions. The cross-method M1/M2/M3 comparison and agent-workload selection
validation were not completed and are stated as limitations in
\S\ref{sec:planned} rather than assumed.
